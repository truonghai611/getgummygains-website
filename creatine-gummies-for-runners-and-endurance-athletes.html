Creatine for runners and endurance athletes

Creatine is not a distance-running performance enhancer in the way it’s a strength and power enhancer — it doesn’t improve VO2 max, submaximal oxygen use, or steady-state time-trial performance. Where the evidence does support it: short, high-intensity efforts within an endurance context, like a finishing kick, a breakaway, or repeated sprint intervals, plus a supportive role in recovery.

If you’re picturing creatine improving your marathon pace directly, the research doesn’t back that up. What it does support is narrower but real: creatine can meaningfully extend how long you can sustain a near-maximal effort — relevant for the final surge in a race, not the steady-state miles before it — along with some evidence for reduced inflammation and faster recovery after hard endurance sessions.

What creatine doesn’t do for endurance

Be precise about this, because it is the part most marketing skips. Creatine supplementation does not increase VO2 max, does not improve submaximal oxygen consumption, and has not shown a benefit for steady-state time-trial performance in controlled research. The mechanism explains why: creatine fuels the phosphagen energy system, which dominates in short, explosive efforts (seconds to about 10 seconds of near-maximal output) — not the aerobic system that dominates sustained distance running.

It has also been suggested that creatine's water-retention effect could theoretically help endurance performance (more fluid, better thermoregulation), but this specific mechanism has been shown to be ineffective in most studies that have actually tested it. Extra bodyweight from water retention, without a corresponding endurance benefit, is a genuine downside worth knowing about if pure distance performance is your only goal.

Where the evidence actually supports it

The more current and specific evidence is for the moments inside an endurance event that are not steady-state — a finishing kick, a breakaway, a hill surge, repeated sprint efforts within a longer race or training session. Research has found creatine supplementation can increase total work sustainable at close to maximal intensity by almost 20 seconds in relevant testing protocols. Since races in many endurance sports are frequently decided in exactly these near-maximal moments (not the aerobic middle section), this is a real, race-relevant benefit for competitive endurance athletes specifically, even though it doesn’t touch the aerobic engine itself.

This also matters for mixed-modality training — interval sessions, hill repeats, track work — where the effort profile is closer to what creatine's mechanism actually supports, versus a steady long run where it has little to offer.

Recovery and cognitive benefits

Two supporting benefits are worth factoring in even if the direct performance case is narrow. There is evidence that creatine can reduce oxidative stress and inflammation following endurance activity, which may support faster recovery between hard sessions — relevant for anyone training endurance events at a meaningful weekly volume. Separately, creatine has shown some cognitive benefit under conditions of sustained mental effort and fatigue, including reduced mental fatigue in at least one study — a real factor in long events where mental fatigue compounds physical fatigue over hours.

Should you actually take it as a runner

The honest answer depends on your event and goals:

  • Sprinters, middle-distance runners, and anyone whose race includes a decisive high-intensity surge (a kick, a breakaway) — the evidence for creatine is more directly relevant.
  • Marathon and ultra-distance runners focused purely on steady-state pace — the direct performance case is weak, and the extra water-retention bodyweight is a genuine tradeoff to weigh against the recovery and interval-training benefits.
  • Anyone doing interval work, hill repeats, or mixed training alongside distance running — the phosphagen-system benefit applies directly to those sessions, even if it doesn't touch your long runs.

If you decide to try it, the standard 3–5 g/day maintenance dose applies the same way as for any other athlete — there is no endurance-specific dosing protocol in the current research.

Questions

Does creatine improve running performance?

Not for steady-state distance performance — it doesn’t improve VO2 max or time-trial pace. It does show benefit for short, near-maximal efforts within an endurance event, like a finishing kick, and for interval-style training.

Will creatine make me slower as a distance runner because of water weight?

It can add 2-5 lbs of water weight initially, which is a real tradeoff for pure distance performance since the endurance benefit from that water retention has not been shown to be effective in most studies. Whether it’s worth it depends on whether your event includes high-intensity surges that creatine’s mechanism actually supports.

Should sprinters and middle-distance runners take creatine?

The evidence is more directly relevant here than for distance running, since these events rely more on the phosphagen energy system that creatine fuels — the same system used in the final surge of many longer races too.

Does creatine help with endurance training recovery even if it doesn’t improve race pace directly?

There is evidence creatine can reduce oxidative stress and inflammation after endurance activity, which may support faster recovery between hard sessions, independent of any direct pace benefit.
