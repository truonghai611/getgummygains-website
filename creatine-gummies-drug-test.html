Is creatine banned? Drug testing explained

No — creatine monohydrate is not a banned substance under WADA, USADA, or NCAA rules, and it's used openly by professional and Olympic athletes. The real risk for a tested athlete isn't creatine itself. It's what else might be in an unverified supplement.

The short version. Creatine monohydrate is legal and unrestricted in essentially every major sport, at any organizational level. The genuine drug-testing risk in this category isn't the creatine — it's supplement contamination, and that's exactly what NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Sport exist to screen for.

Creatine's actual legal status in sport

Creatine monohydrate is not on the World Anti-Doping Agency's Prohibited List, is not banned by USADA, and is not restricted by the NCAA. It is one of the most widely used and openly discussed supplements among professional and Olympic athletes precisely because it carries no doping classification — this has been true for decades and is not a gray area.

So why does "NSF Certified for Sport" matter at all?

Because the actual risk isn't the labeled ingredient — it's undeclared contamination. Supplement manufacturing facilities that also produce other products can have cross-contamination with actually banned substances (stimulants, anabolic agents) that never appear on the label. This has caused real, documented positive drug tests in athletes who took a contaminated supplement without knowing it. NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Sport both test finished products specifically for this kind of contamination, in addition to confirming label accuracy — which is a different and additional layer of testing beyond just "does it contain what it says."

The practical takeaway for tested athletes: creatine itself is not the concern. An unverified supplement from a facility with no contamination testing is the concern, because you have no way to know what else might be in it. If you compete in any drug-tested context, choosing a product with NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport status is about contamination risk, not creatine's legality.

What to actually check

  • Search the NSF Certified for Sport database or the Informed Sport product list directly for the specific product — not just the brand name, since formulations and facilities can differ across a brand's product line.
  • Don't rely on vague "third-party tested" language that doesn't name a specific, searchable program — see our brand reviews for examples of this exact gap.
  • Check with your sport's governing body if you have any doubt — rules can vary and this article is general information, not a substitute for your organization's specific policy.
