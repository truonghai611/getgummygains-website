Creatine's actual legal status in sport

Creatine monohydrate is not named on the World Anti-Doping Agency Prohibited List, and USADA follows that list. It is not an NCAA banned drug. The list changes, so the check that matters is the current year’s list, not this paragraph. A gummy is still a manufactured supplement: the prohibited-list status of creatine does not clear the rest of the tub.

So why does "NSF Certified for Sport" matter at all?

Because the actual risk isn't the labeled ingredient — it's undeclared contamination. Supplement manufacturing facilities that also produce other products can have cross-contamination with actually banned substances (stimulants, anabolic agents) that never appear on the label. This has caused real, documented positive drug tests in athletes who took a contaminated supplement without knowing it. NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Sport both test finished products specifically for this kind of contamination, in addition to confirming label accuracy — which is a different and additional layer of testing beyond just "does it contain what it says."

The practical takeaway for tested athletes: creatine itself is not the concern. An unverified supplement from a facility with no contamination testing is the concern, because you have no way to know what else might be in it. If you compete in any drug-tested context, choosing a product with NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport status is about contamination risk, not creatine's legality.

Is creatine legal?

Yes. Creatine monohydrate is a legal dietary supplement in the United States and is not a controlled substance. It is not on the WADA Prohibited List, and no major sporting body bans it as an ingredient. The confusion usually comes from a different question — whether a specific product is safe for a tested athlete — which is not about creatine at all.

Is creatine banned by the NCAA?

It is not an NCAA banned drug. Creatine is not on the list of drug classes the NCAA tests for. A different rule covers what a school may supply. Division I Bylaw 16.5.2 allows an institution to provide only named classes of nutritional supplements, such as carbohydrate and electrolyte drinks, energy bars, protein, and vitamins and minerals, and those products still cannot contain an NCAA banned substance. Creatine is outside those classes. Older NCAA staff guidance listed creatine and creatine-containing compounds as not permissible for the school to provide. That is a rule about the institution, not a positive drug test for the athlete. Confirm the current bylaw with your compliance office. The NCAA does not certify products as “NCAA compliant.”

Bylaw text: NCAA LSDBi, Bylaw 16.5.2 (reference date August 9, 2024). Supplement categories: NCAA dietary supplement fact sheet.

Is creatine banned in the US military?

Creatine monohydrate is not on the DoD Prohibited Dietary Supplement Ingredients list. Operation Supplement Safety, run for the Department of Defense, says so on its creatine page, and a September 25, 2025 Defense Health Agency information paper says service members are permitted to use creatine because it is not on that list. DoD Instruction 6130.06 is the policy that tells service members not to use ingredients on the list. The prohibited-ingredient database OPSS hosts was marked last updated September 3, 2026.

That is an ingredient rule, not a brand approval. DoD does not publish a list of banned or approved supplement brands. A gummy can still be off limits if another ingredient on the Supplement Facts panel is prohibited, or if it is a drug ingredient that does not belong in a dietary supplement. OPSS also says to use creatine with guidance from a healthcare provider or a performance dietitian, and that creatine monohydrate is the form with the research behind it.

OPSS: creatine monohydrate · DoD prohibited dietary supplement ingredients · DHA information paper, September 25, 2025 · DoDI 6130.06 (March 9, 2022).

So what is the actual risk for a tested athlete?

Contamination, not creatine. A supplement can pick up a banned substance from shared manufacturing equipment or from a poorly controlled raw material, and under strict liability the athlete is responsible for what is in their body regardless of how it got there. That is the entire reason the certification programmes exist.

What the registry actually covers. NSF Certified for Sport screens each certified batch for label accuracy and for more than 280 substances banned in sport, and publishes the result in a database anyone can search. Across the eleven creatine gummy brands we have checked, the registry returns 50 certified creatine listings, concentrated in five brands: Create Wellness (25), Momentous (8), Beast Bites (8), Force Factor (6) and Lemme (3). Legion, Bloom, SWOLY, Wild Gainz, Bounce and Bear Balanced returned none (checked 13 September 2026).

The practical rule for anyone subject to testing: search the registry for your exact product and flavour before every reorder, not once. Certification attaches to a specific product and can lapse, and brands add and remove flavours constantly.
