Is creatine gluten-free?

The creatine itself, always. The product you are holding, not necessarily — and the difference is the whole answer. Here is what is actually at risk, and which brands say anything about it.

The headline number: creatine monohydrate is a single compound containing no wheat, barley or rye — it is inherently gluten-free, in powder, capsule or gummy form. Of the nine gummy brands we track, however, only one states gluten-free on its product page. The risk was never the creatine; it is everything the manufacturer adds around it.

Why pure creatine cannot contain gluten

Gluten is a protein found in wheat, barley and rye. Creatine monohydrate is not derived from any of them — it is synthesised industrially from sarcosine and cyanamide, and the finished molecule is a single defined compound. There is no botanical route by which gluten can be present in the creatine itself.

That is why every reputable source answers this question “yes” and moves on. The problem is that the question people are actually asking is not about creatine.

What people are really asking

Someone searching this is almost never holding a bag of raw creatine powder. They are holding a flavoured product — a gummy, a flavoured powder, a pre-workout blend — and the gluten question applies to the other twelve things on the ingredient list, plus the facility it was made in. Three real routes:

  • Added ingredients. Flavourings, colourings, thickeners and malt-derived sweeteners can carry gluten. Barley malt is the one to watch in flavoured products.
  • Shared equipment. A facility that also runs wheat-based products can cross-contaminate, which is why the FDA gluten-free labelling rule sets a threshold (under 20 ppm) rather than requiring zero.
  • Nobody checked. A brand that has never tested cannot tell you either way, and absence of a claim is not a claim.

For a preference versus for coeliac disease

If you avoid gluten by choice, plain creatine monohydrate powder settles the question completely — one ingredient, nothing to go wrong. If you have coeliac disease or non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, the standard is different: you want an explicit gluten-free statement on the product you are buying, and ideally a brand in a third-party certification programme, because that is the only route that involves someone outside the company looking at the product.

What we found when we checked. Across the nine creatine gummy brands we track, Bear Balanced is the only one that states gluten-free outright on its product page (checked 13 September 2026). Create and Beast Bites are pectin-based and vegan — which answers the gelatin question, not the gluten one — and neither publishes a gluten statement where a shopper would look. That is not evidence of gluten; it is evidence that nobody has told you.

The two-minute check before you buy

In order, and it takes less time than reading a review: read the full ingredient list for malt, barley or unspecified “natural flavours” on a product that does not state gluten-free; look for an explicit gluten-free statement rather than a vegan or non-GMO one; and search the product in the NSF Certified for Sport registry, because a brand in an ongoing certification programme has at minimum submitted to outside scrutiny of what is in the pack.

Bottom line. Creatine is gluten-free. Creatine gummies are gluten-free if the maker says so and has something to back it — and most of them say nothing at all. Questions

Is creatine gluten-free?

Yes. Creatine monohydrate is a single compound containing no wheat, barley or rye, so it is inherently gluten-free. Any gluten risk in a finished product comes from added flavourings, sweeteners, colourings or shared manufacturing equipment — never from the creatine itself.

Does creatine contain gluten?

Pure creatine monohydrate does not. Flavoured creatine products can, through added ingredients such as malt-derived sweeteners or through cross-contamination in a facility that also handles wheat. The FDA gluten-free labelling rule sets a threshold below 20 ppm rather than requiring absolute zero.

Are creatine gummies gluten-free?

Only if the brand says so. Of the nine creatine gummy brands we track, Bear Balanced is the only one stating gluten-free on its product page as of 13 September 2026. Create and Beast Bites are pectin-based and vegan, which addresses gelatin rather than gluten, and publish no gluten statement.

Is creatine safe with coeliac disease?

The creatine is. The product may not be, depending on what else is in it and where it was made. For coeliac disease the practical standard is an explicit gluten-free statement on the specific product, not an inference from the fact that creatine is gluten-free. Unflavoured creatine monohydrate powder removes the question entirely.

Does vegan or non-GMO mean gluten-free?

No. Vegan means no animal-derived ingredients — in gummies it usually means pectin instead of gelatin. Non-GMO refers to genetic modification. Neither says anything about wheat, barley or rye, and brands frequently publish those two claims while staying silent on gluten.

Is creatine powder safer than gummies for gluten?

Simpler, rather than safer. Unflavoured creatine monohydrate powder has one ingredient, so there is nothing to check beyond the facility. A gummy has ten or more, which is where the uncertainty enters. If gluten avoidance is medical rather than a preference, the shorter ingredient list is the easier product to verify.
