Creatine gummies vs. capsules: which should you buy?

The creatine molecule doesn't care which format delivers it. The real differences are what you pay per gram, how much sugar comes along, and how many units you need to swallow or chew.

Short answer. Gummies and capsules both deliver creatine monohydrate at over 99% bioavailability — the science is identical. Capsules are usually cheaper per gram and add no sugar. Gummies cost more and require checking the label carefully, but win on taste for people who can't tolerate powder or swallowing pills.

Bioavailability: is there actually a difference?

No, not in any way that matters. Once a capsule shell dissolves in your stomach — typically within minutes — the creatine inside behaves identically to creatine from a gummy or a glass of powder. Bioavailability for all three formats is routinely cited above 99%. The only measurable difference is speed: powder reaches your system a little faster since there's no shell to dissolve first, and a gummy sits somewhere in between depending on how quickly it breaks down while chewing.

That timing gap — minutes, not hours — is irrelevant for a supplement that works by gradually saturating muscle creatine stores over three to four weeks of daily use. None of these formats has a meaningful edge on actual results.

Dose accuracy: neither format is "one and done"

A common assumption is that capsules are simpler because you "just take a pill." In practice, capsules usually carry 700 mg to 1 g of creatine each, which means hitting a real 3–5 g daily target still takes 3 to 5 capsules — a multi-unit count much closer to gummies than people expect. The difference isn't "one pill vs. several gummies"; it's several capsules with no sugar vs. several gummies with some.

Whichever format you choose, always check total creatine per full serving on the supplement facts panel, not the count printed on the front of the bottle. See our dosage guide for the exact math either format requires.

Where gummies still make sense

If you've tried powder or capsules and reliably stopped taking them — because of the taste, the mixing hassle, or difficulty swallowing pills — a gummy that passes independent lab testing isn't a bad choice. Creatine only works if you take it consistently for weeks, and the format that gets you to actually do that beats the format that's cheapest on paper but sits unused in a cabinet. The catch is the same one that applies everywhere on this site: verify the dose with third-party testing before you commit (see our lab test results).

Bottom line. Chemically, gummies and capsules are equivalent delivery vehicles for creatine. Capsules are the more efficient choice on cost and sugar. Gummies are a reasonable, if pricier, choice if that format is genuinely what keeps you consistent — as long as you check the real dose and buy a verified brand.

Common questions

Do creatine capsules work as well as gummies?

Yes. Both formats deliver creatine monohydrate at over 99% bioavailability once dissolved. The capsule shell adds a few minutes to absorption versus a gummy or powder, which is irrelevant given creatine works through weeks of gradual muscle saturation, not an acute effect.

Are creatine capsules cheaper than gummies?

Usually, yes, per gram of creatine. Capsules are closer to encapsulated powder with less manufacturing overhead than a flavored, textured gummy, so they typically cost less for the same daily dose.

How many capsules equal a full creatine dose?

Most capsules carry 700 mg–1 g of creatine each, so reaching a 3–5 g daily target usually takes 3 to 5 capsules — a similar multi-unit count to gummies, just without the sugar.

Which format should I actually choose?

If cost and simplicity matter most, capsules are the more efficient choice. If you know from experience that a candy-like format is the only thing that gets you to take a supplement daily, a verified gummy is a reasonable trade of a higher price for better adherence.
