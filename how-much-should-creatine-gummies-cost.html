The math nobody puts on the label

Price per bottle is close to meaningless without knowing servings per bottle, and servings per bottle depends on gummy count and creatine per gummy — both of which vary a lot. A 90-gummy tub sounds bigger than a 60-gummy one, but if the 90-gummy tub needs 3 gummies per serving (30 servings) and the 60-gummy one needs 5 (12 servings), the gap is far wider than the counts on the front suggest. Sizes also change without warning: Create replaced its 45-gummy bottle with a 90-count tub and a 30-count pouch during 2026, which moved every per-serving number on this page.

The only reliable comparison is: total price ÷ number of full servings the bottle actually contains, where a "full serving" means enough gummies to hit your 3–5 g daily target — not the brand's printed "serving size" if that serving size under-delivers.

Why the spread is so wide

A few real, non-arbitrary reasons a certified gummy can cost more than double another:

  • Certification and testing. An ongoing NSF Certified for Sport program — which tests batches for label accuracy and over 280 banned substances — costs the brand real, recurring money that gets built into the price. A brand skipping this can price lower.
  • Creatine source. Some brands use a specific patented creatine source (like Creapure®) with its own certification and cost structure, versus generic bulk creatine monohydrate.
  • Piece count and manufacturing complexity. Getting more creatine into a single gummy (like 1.5 g/piece vs. 0.8 g/piece) without compromising texture is a harder manufacturing problem, and that R&D cost shows up in price.
  • Sugar and flavor formulation. Sugar-free or low-sugar versions using sugar alcohols instead of standard sugar typically cost more to produce.

What actually represents good value

Given the category-wide testing problem — independent testing has found roughly half of tested gummy brands failed to meet their label claim — the highest-value purchase is the cheapest option with a confirmed, searchable certification, not the cheapest option full stop. On that basis, Force Factor (~$1.00/serving, confirmed NSF Certified for Sport) is the strongest budget pick we found, and Create (~$1.60/serving through our link) is a featured three-gummy option; Force Factor also has a published passing sample result, so that evidence is not unique to Create.

The rule that saves you money in this category: verification first, price second. An unverified $1.50 gummy that under-delivers on dose is a worse deal than a verified $2.00 gummy that delivers exactly what it says.

Questions

What is a fair price for creatine gummies?

Among verified, certified brands, roughly $1.00 to $2.50 per serving is the real range. Products priced meaningfully below that with no confirmed certification are a higher risk, not necessarily a better deal.

Why are creatine gummies so much more expensive than powder?

Bulk creatine powder runs roughly $0.20 per 5 g dose, versus $1.00-$2.50 for an equivalent gummy serving — an 5x to 12x premium. You're paying for manufacturing complexity, flavoring, testing/certification, and convenience, not a chemically different or superior ingredient.

Is the cheapest creatine gummy ever the best deal?

Rarely, in this specific category. Independent testing has found close to half of tested gummy brands don't meet their label's creatine claim. An underdosed cheap gummy is a full-price purchase for a fraction of the actual dose.

How do I calculate the true price per serving?

Divide the total bottle price by the number of full servings it actually contains, where a full serving means the number of gummies needed to reach your 3-5 g daily target — not necessarily the brand's printed serving size, if that serving under-delivers.
