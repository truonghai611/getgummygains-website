The real difference: chewing vs. mixing

Both formats exist to remove the same friction as a tub of powder — no scoop, no shaker, no measuring at the moment you take it. Where they actually differ is what they ask of you and what they cost in sugar, price and precision.

So which should you buy?

Buy gummies if the real failure point in your routine is that you will not do any mixing step at all — not even tearing a packet into a water bottle. Convenience is the entire value proposition, so it only pays off if it is the reason you stay consistent.

Buy stick packs if you want the full research dose without added sugar, you already carry a water bottle or coffee most days, and the few seconds of stirring is not the thing that makes you skip a dose.

Both only deliver on that promise if the brand actually contains what it claims — check our lab test results before trusting either format's label.

Questions people actually ask

Are stick packs cheaper than gummies?

Usually, yes. Because a stick pack typically delivers a full 5 g dose per packet with no added sugar, you are not paying the piece-count and sugar-formulation costs that gummies carry. Always check the real cost per 5 g dose rather than the price per box, since pack sizes vary.

Do stick packs work as well as gummies for travel?

Both are TSA-friendly and pre-portioned. The difference is that stick packs need a liquid to mix into, while gummies need nothing. If you always have a water bottle or coffee with you when traveling, a stick pack is just as convenient; if you sometimes do not, gummies have the edge.

Can I mix a stick pack into anything besides water?

Most unflavored creatine monohydrate dissolves fine in water, coffee, juice or a protein shake without altering taste meaningfully, since creatine itself is close to flavorless. Flavored stick packs are formulated for water specifically and may taste off in other drinks.

Which format has better dose accuracy?

Neither format guarantees accuracy just by design — both depend on the brand actually containing its labeled amount. In independent testing, gummies had the worse track record: roughly half of tested gummy brands failed to meet label claim, while powders (the base format stick packs use) had a much stronger pass rate.
